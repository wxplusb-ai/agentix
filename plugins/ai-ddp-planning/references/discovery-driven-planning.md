# Discovery-Driven Planning

The planning philosophy behind every AI-AD&D deliverable. Read this before working on any step.

## Why conventional planning fails here

Conventional plans project the future from the past and hold people to the numbers. That works when the work is familiar. AI applications are not familiar work: model behavior is probabilistic, data quality is unknown until it is examined, and users' willingness to trust an agent is unknown until they try one. A plan built on confident estimates in that situation treats assumptions as facts, which McGrath and MacMillan identify as the single most common cause of failed ventures.

Discovery-driven planning accepts that the future is uncertain and plans to learn. The plan is organized around converting the most consequential assumptions into knowledge at the least cost, before making commitments that are expensive to reverse.

## The assumption-to-knowledge ratio

In any new venture, the proportion of assumptions to knowledge is high. The higher that ratio, the more disciplined the team must be about knowing which assumptions it holds, testing them, and changing the plan when evidence arrives. People forget they made assumptions, forget why, and fail to see how one wrong assumption ripples through the rest of the plan. Writing beliefs down and testing them is the cure.

## Beliefs, bets and knowledge

- **Knowledge** is something supported by evidence the client already has: data, interviews, a working prototype, signed customers.
- **A belief** is something the client holds to be true without that evidence. Most of a new AI application's plan rests on beliefs.
- **A bet** is a belief that is both highly consequential (the project fails or changes shape if it is wrong) and weakly supported. The bets are what the plan must test first. A bet with high consequence and no evidence at all is a **leap of faith**.

## The five disciplines

1. **Frame.** Specify what success looks like at maturity, in unambiguous terms, so everyone knows whether the effort is worth it. If the upside is not attractive, nothing else matters.
2. **Benchmark against reality.** Check the plan against what competitors, alternatives and markets actually support. Plans on paper tend to assume better technology, larger markets and weaker competition than reality offers.
3. **Translate strategy into deliverables.** Work backward from what must be delivered to get the result, rather than forward from what the team is used to doing.
4. **Document, test and revisit assumptions.** This is the core difference from conventional planning. Every assumption is written down, rated, and assigned a way to be tested.
5. **Plan to milestones.** A milestone is a point where many assumptions can be tested and much is learned: a feasibility result, a concept test, first customer use. Plan in detail to the next milestone, not beyond it.

## Experiments

An experiment is the way a bet gets tested: a piece of work whose result confirms the bet, refutes it, or reshapes it. Good experiments are small, time-boxed, decisive and cheap to be wrong about. In AI-AD&D, experiments are not separate items in the plan. They are carried out through catalog nodes (an AI Feasibility Spike, a Customer Problem Validation, a Solution Concept Validation and so on), and they are the main reason nodes are grouped into phases and ordered the way they are. An experiment may be carried by part of one phase, a whole phase, or several phases.

## What this means for every agent

- Treat every claim in the client's material as a belief until it is backed by evidence.
- Put the work that tests the riskiest bets as early as dependencies allow.
- Plan in detail only as far as current knowledge supports.
- Never present an approximation as if it were a commitment.
